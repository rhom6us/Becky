## 1

It was a Monday like any Monday. 1997. Grey sky, grey city, grey coffee going cold on the counter. Francois had been away for the weekend — business, he'd say later, though nobody asked twice. He stepped inside. He looked at the door to the next room. Something was *deeply, fundamentally wrong* in a way that a man feels in his chest before his brain has caught up. Francois's legs went out from under him. He grabbed the nearest bedpost and held on like a man clinging to a life raft in a black ocean.

[DEV: Strong opening. The triptych of grey establishes the genre register instantly. "Something was deeply, fundamentally wrong in a way that a man feels in his chest before his brain has caught up" is doing the right preparatory work — we know what Francois is about to find without being told. The image of the clinging is well-placed.]

![Stripes clinging to the bedpost](../../images/600-ice-48005.jpg)

He called the police with trembling fingers.

They arrived within the hour — two uniforms, bored on a Monday, expecting a noise complaint or a missing wallet. What they found was Francois, still attached to the furniture, eyes wild, unable to speak in full sentences.

"In there," he managed. "The next room. She's — I can't — just go look."

[DEV: The fragmentary speech here is the right texture for a man in genuine shock. Note that this is also the only window we get into Francois as a suffering person rather than a puzzle — this moment of fragmentation is doing double duty as character and as structural contrast with his controlled, strategic behavior in Section 2.]

They looked.

She was on the floor. Face down. Arms out. Not moving. Not breathing. A stuffed ostrich in a teal collar who went by the name Vivian Plume, and who would never again make anyone's acquaintance.

[DEV: This is Vivian's formal introduction, and it arrives through the officers' discovery of her body. The teal collar and the name are good details — specific, humanizing. The closing phrase "would never again make anyone's acquaintance" sits at a calibration point: it's dry and elegant as a phrasing, but some of the weight of it lands as a kicker rather than as mourning. The story does properly mourn Vivian later (Section 5's reflection, the coda's burial). Whether the introduction should carry more of that weight is a judgment call — noir convention supports the dry register, and the distance here may be doing appropriate work given that we're in the officers' POV for this beat.]

The officers exchanged a glance.

![Vivian Plume face down on the carpet](../../images/600-ice-48006.jpg)

The lead officer snapped his notebook shut. "We'll file a report," he said, in the tone of a man who was already thinking about lunch.

[DEV: "Thinking about lunch" is exactly right — dismissive, petty, true to the type. This moment of institutional indifference is the inciting incident's second half: not just Vivian's death, but the certainty that official channels will not serve justice. The logic that sends Francois to Tom is sound.]

Francois knew then that the police weren't up to this. This wasn't a case for procedure, for forms in triplicate, for men who solved things with clipboards. This case required a different kind of mind. A darker one. One that had stared into the abyss long enough that the abyss had started leaving him voicemails.

[DEV: "The abyss had started leaving him voicemails" is a nice tonal flourish — establishes the register's awareness of its own conventions without breaking the frame. The segue into Tom via Francois's reasoning is efficient and sufficient.]

There was only one man.

PI Tom.

## 2

Tom heard him out without expression. Then he said no.

Francois asked why.

Tom didn't answer that. What he didn't say — what he wouldn't say to a client, or a stranger, or frankly anyone — was that he'd been hearing things for the better part of a year. Whispers, mostly. The kind that travel through the back channels of the city, cop to cop, case to cold case, spoken quietly over bad coffee in rooms with the door closed. Something was going around. Something that got into people. Not a sickness, not exactly — more like a *reorientation.* Men and women who by all accounts had been perfectly ordinary, perfectly decent, until one day they simply weren't anymore. The ones who could still talk afterwards struggled to describe the before and after. The ones who couldn't talk anymore were the ones who kept Tom up at night. He glanced at Francois — the too-wide eyes, the hands clasped just a little too tight.

[DEV: This is the story's most efficient piece of exposition — it establishes the corruption, Tom's existing knowledge of it, and his awareness that something is wrong with Francois, all in a single interior paragraph. The specific detail of "bad coffee in rooms with the door closed" gives the whisper network a texture. The structural elegance: Tom's refusal is grounded in knowledge the reader is receiving simultaneously. We refuse alongside him.]

He'd steered clear of anything that smelled like it for months. And something about the shape of this case — a senseless death, a man clinging to furniture, eyes that were just slightly too wide — smelled exactly like it.

So he said no.

But Francois was persistent. He leaned in, voice low and urgent, chipping away — planting small seeds of doubt, little splinters of argument that lodged themselves just under the skin — "What if the police botch it? What if justice is never served? Where is the hope, and where is the faith? What if the truth just disappears, like she did?"[^angels]

[DEV: Author confirms intentional — F1 lyric bleed embedded in the persuasion sequence. The phrasing "Where is the hope, and where is the faith?" is the first detectable bleed for a close reader; it scans differently from the surrounding rhetorical questions. The narrative does not flag it explicitly, which is the right choice. The progression of questions here mirrors Francois's actual psychological state — he is both arguing and fragmenting.]

He stopped. "Don't try too hard to think," he said. "Don't —"[^staring] He paused. Seemed to lose it briefly. "If there's an order in all of this disorder — I just need to know what it is."[^wake]

[DEV: Author confirms intentional — F2 lyric bleed, presented as mid-sentence interruption. The loss of coherence in the pause is the right texture. The second sentence ("If there's an order in all of this disorder") is the story's most overtly fragmentary bleed — it sounds like a plea that predates the conversation. The dramatic irony is that Francois is describing the chaos in his own mind while nominally talking about the case. Strong.]

Then, just for a moment — half a second, maybe less — something crossed his face. Not grief, not urgency. Something that looked, just briefly, almost like terror. Then it was gone, and he was back, hands clasped, eyes searching Tom's face like nothing had happened.

[DEV: Author confirms intentional — the flash of terror is the uncorrupted fragment surfacing momentarily (per CLAUDE.md). The suppression of it ("Then it was gone, and he was back") is handled correctly — the reader registers something Tom registers, but neither the character nor the narration explains it. This is the story's first planted seed for the twist.]

Tom filed it away — the thing he couldn't name. He'd spent twenty years developing an instinct, and the instinct was making noise. Francois said all the right things in all the right order, and his devastation seemed genuine, and none of that stopped the quiet alarm going off somewhere below Tom's conscious mind. A man in genuine shock didn't argue this well. A man in genuine shock didn't land his points this cleanly. Tom had the distinct and unverifiable sense of being steered.

[DEV: "The distinct and unverifiable sense of being steered" is a precise articulation of the story's central dramatic irony. The narration is doing subtle work: Tom is right that he is being steered, but he cannot know yet by what (the fragment, not the corruption). This sentence will reward a second reading after the twist.]

The other thing — smaller, harder to name — was the rhythm of some of it. The phrasing. Every so often Francois would say something and it would scan differently — like he was receiving the words rather than choosing them. Too even. A little too complete. Like a line from a song, almost — like it had a life that predated the conversation it arrived in.

[DEV: This is the story's explicit foreshadowing of the lyric bleed mechanic — Tom notices the anomalous rhythm without being able to name it. The phrase "a life that predated the conversation it arrived in" is the cleanest articulation of what the lyric bleed is. One structural note: this is the third consecutive paragraph of Tom's interior observation (the alarm, the sense of being steered, the rhythm). The clustering works because the observations are escalating, but be aware that a reader who is not tracking closely may lose the thread of the escalation. This is not a problem at the current level of prose compression; flagging for awareness.]

He should have walked away. He knew he should have walked away.

"He just needs to know who did this," Francois said. "Francois deserves that much."

[DEV: Author confirms intentional — the third-person slip ("Francois deserves that much" instead of "I deserve that much") is planted here per CLAUDE.md. Tom files it. The story never explains it in this scene — the reader has to catch it themselves, which is correct. One note: the slip is relatively easy to miss because it follows immediately from "He just needs to know" — a sentence that could read as Francois speaking in the third person for rhetorical emphasis. The proximity of the two third-person references may slightly soften the uncanniness of the slip. Whether this matters depends on how clearly you want the slip to register. If it's meant to be subtle (a seed, not a flag), the current placement is fine.]

Tom sat with his fist pressed hard against his chin, eyes fixed on the middle distance. Two alarm systems going off at once. The instinct. The rumors. He turned the shape of the thing over, looking for the angle that held, looking for the solid ground of reason in a conversation that kept moving the floor on him.

[DEV: "A conversation that kept moving the floor on him" is the right metaphor — it locates the reader in Tom's physical disorientation without being explicit. The naming of "the instinct" and "the rumors" here will be echoed in the Section 5 corridor callback. Consider whether the naming here should be more prominent (as it is) or slightly less prominent, depending on how much weight you want the callback to carry. If the callback is the first time a reader consciously registers the phrase "two alarm systems," it hits harder. If they've already named them here, the callback functions as confirmation rather than revelation. Both are valid choices; just be intentional.]

![Tom fist to chin](../../images/600-ice-48004.jpg)

Finally, something gave.

"Fine," he said. "But I do this my way."

[DEV: The capitulation is structurally correct — it comes after the image (which functions as a pause beat) and after Tom has visibly worked the problem. The "my way" condition is a small assertion of control that character-codes Tom efficiently: he knows he's compromised his judgment, and this is how he lives with it. Strong close for the section.]

## 3

Tom worked the case for three days straight. He followed every thread until the threads led somewhere ugly. And they always led somewhere ugly.

[DEV: Effective compression. The three days of investigation are summarized in two sentences, which is the right structural decision for this length — dramatizing the investigation would unbalance the proportions. The repetition of "ugly" is deliberate and consistent with the register. No note.]

Everyone he talked to said Vivian had been worried about Francois for weeks. Nobody had done anything about it, including her. He went through the rooms on the second day. The bed with its missing occupant. A boombox. Scissors. A coffee cup. Three days of unopened mail. Expecting nothing. What he found was a folded piece of paper in her desk drawer — not a diary, not a letter. A list. Eight or nine lines in her handwriting, each one something Francois had said. No dates. No context. Just the words, because the words had been strange enough to write down. Most of them Tom couldn't place. But near the bottom, underlined twice, with a question mark beside it: *You know you're chewing bubble gum.*[^discotheque]

[DEV: Vivian's list is one of the story's most efficient moments. In a single beat it: (1) confirms that the lyric bleed preceded the murder by weeks, (2) establishes that Vivian was paying attention and was frightened, (3) gives Vivian retroactive interiority and credibility as a person rather than a plot object, and (4) hands us the first textually confirmed lyric fragment. The detail "underlined twice, with a question mark beside it" is exact — it tells us everything about how Vivian held this strange phrase in her mind. The inventory of the room ("The bed with its missing occupant. A boombox. Scissors. A coffee cup.") is economical and creates the right sense of a space emptied out by violence and absence. No note.]

He looked at it for a long time. He thought about a woman writing down the things her partner said because they had started to frighten her — not knowing why, not knowing what to do with the list once she had it. He thought about the better part of a year he'd spent listening to the city whisper about something that rewrote people quietly, patiently, until one day the words coming out of their mouths weren't theirs anymore.

[DEV: This is the story's clearest moment of emotional register for both Tom and for Vivian — Tom's interiority here reads as genuine rather than analytical. "Not knowing what to do with the list once she had it" is the story's most poignant line about Vivian, and it works because it's true to the limitation of a person who sees something wrong and has no framework for addressing it. The thematic resonance with Tom's own position is present (he also knows something is wrong and has no framework for fully addressing it) without being stated. Good restraint.]

He folded the paper and put it in his pocket.

He turned to the boombox.

It sat on the bed like an altar. A U2 CD loaded in the tray. Tom stood over it, not moving. He knew this smell. He knew what the boombox was. He knew what the boombox had done. He knew what pressing play would mean.

[DEV: The repetition of "He knew" (four times in close succession) is functioning as deliberate incantation — the rhythm mimics the act of convincing yourself of something you already know. It also sets up the line that follows with maximum impact, because we have just been told, repeatedly, that he knew what pressing play would mean. The effect is correct.]

His hand moved before he'd decided.

[DEV: This is the most structurally important sentence in the manuscript. Tom's first major failure of will — the action that will make the corridor question possible — is delivered in seven words with no psychological lead-up. The compression is either the story's greatest risk or its greatest precision, depending on the reader. For some readers, this will land as inevitable (the hand moves; of course it does; we knew he would); for others, it may feel too fast to fully absorb. It is absolutely in register for noir — the genre regularly stages failures of will this way — and adjusting it toward more explicit psychological preparation would damage the pacing. Flagging it as a high-stakes compression point. Trust the choice.]

He listened for forty seconds. Then he pressed stop and stood very still for a long moment.

*So it was true.*

He didn't press play again.

[DEV: "So it was true" as an italicised interior beat is the right close for this moment — it's confirmation, not conclusion. The italics signal something Tom is saying to himself rather than narrating. The refusal to press play again is the story's first small assertion of Tom's will, which sets up the corridor question: was that refusal his, or something else's? This paragraph, in retrospect, is deeply ambiguous.]

The picture became clear. Francois had found the album. Had listened — once, probably, just to see what all the fuss was about. That's how it always started. Just once. And then the music had gotten into him, into the soft tissue behind his eyes, and it had begun its quiet, patient work. Rewriting things. Darkening corners. Turning a reasonable man into something that could look at Vivian — innocent Vivian — and decide she knew too much.

[DEV: "Innocent Vivian" is a small, important acknowledgment — it's the closest Tom comes to mourning her during the investigation, and it arrives embedded in logic rather than in feeling. The phrase "soft tissue behind his eyes" is a good image for where the corruption lives. The reasoning here is clean. No note.]

Because she did know. She'd been there when he first listened. She'd seen what it was doing to him. And a corrupted mind cannot abide a witness.

The conclusion was impossible. Tom ran the logic again. And again. Same answer, every time.

He went back to Francois.
