# **Forty Seconds**

*Written by Thomas Butler*

*Pictures by Rebecca Stewart*

*Edited by Ruth-Ann Butler*

## 1

It was a Monday like any Monday. 1997. Grey sky, grey city, grey coffee going cold on the counter. The kind of morning where even the pigeons looked like they'd rather be somewhere else. Rain had been threatening since dawn but hadn't committed — just a low, flat sky pressing down on the rooftops like a hand on a lid.

Francois had been away for the weekend — business, he'd say later, though nobody asked twice. He stepped inside. The apartment was quiet in a way it shouldn't have been. Not empty-quiet — apartments are empty all the time and they don't feel like this. This was the quiet of something having happened. He looked at the door to the next room. Something was *deeply, fundamentally wrong* in a way that a man feels in his chest before his brain has caught up. Francois's legs went out from under him. He grabbed the nearest bedpost and held on like a man clinging to a life raft in a black ocean.

![Stripes clinging to the bedpost](../../images/600-ice-48005.jpg)

He called the police with trembling fingers.

They arrived within the hour — two uniforms, bored on a Monday, expecting a noise complaint or a missing wallet. They came up the stairs with the unhurried gait of men who had done this a thousand times and expected to do it a thousand more. The younger one had a coffee stain on his sleeve. The older one had the face of a man counting the days to his pension.

What they found was Francois, still attached to the furniture, eyes wild, unable to speak in full sentences.

"In there," he managed. "The next room. She's — I can't — just go look."

They looked.

She was on the floor. Face down. Arms out. Not moving. Not breathing. A stuffed ostrich in a teal collar who went by the name Vivian Plume, and who would never again make anyone's acquaintance.

The officers exchanged a glance.

![Vivian Plume face down on the carpet](../../images/600-ice-48006.jpg)

The lead officer snapped his notebook shut. "We'll file a report," he said, in the tone of a man who was already thinking about lunch.

His partner lingered. Looked at Francois. Looked back at the body. Looked at the distance between the two rooms — short hallway, no locked doors, nothing between the living and the dead but ten feet of carpet. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Followed his partner out.

They left the way they came. The door shut behind them with a sound that was neither loud nor soft, just final. Outside, the sky stayed grey. A car passed. A dog barked three blocks over, the sound carrying further than it should have in the heavy air. The building settled back into its silence. Francois sat on the floor with his back against the bedpost and stared at the wall opposite and did not move for what might have been five minutes or might have been an hour.

Francois knew then that the police weren't up to this. This wasn't a case for procedure, for forms in triplicate, for men who solved things with clipboards. This case required a different kind of mind. A darker one. One that would look at the impossible shape of the thing and not flinch. One that had stared into the abyss long enough that the abyss had started leaving him voicemails.

There was only one man.

PI Tom.

---

The city, in 1997, was a place that kept its secrets badly. You could feel them in the air — not the secrets themselves but the weight of them, the way a room feels different when someone's been crying in it. The buildings knew. The streets knew. The rain, which fell most days with the reliable indifference of a thing that had been falling a long time and expected to keep falling, knew. It was a city of closed doors and drawn curtains and conversations that stopped when you walked in and started again when you left, and if you'd asked any of its residents whether something was wrong, they'd have said no, and they'd have meant it, and they'd have been lying.

## 2

Tom's office sat above a dry cleaner's on the east side of the city, in the kind of building that didn't so much have character as have a rap sheet. The stairs creaked at intervals designed to announce visitors before they arrived. The hallway smelled like solvents and regret. His door had a pane of frosted glass with his name on it — PI TOM — and a deadbolt he replaced every six months because the neighbourhood kept finding ways to improve upon the last one.

He was sitting behind his desk when Francois walked in. No appointment. No knock. Just a zebra in his doorway, breathing hard, looking like he hadn't slept in days.

Tom noted, without entirely meaning to, that two separate alarm systems had failed to go off. The deadbolt hadn't caught. The stairs hadn't creaked. Francois had simply appeared — as though the building had decided, without consulting Tom, to let him in.

He filed it. Didn't think about it. Not yet.

He let Francois sit. Didn't offer coffee. Didn't offer anything. Tom had a face built for listening — broad, patient, as expressive as a brick wall. Twenty years of other people's worst days had worn it smooth. He'd heard confessions, alibis, threats, proposals, and once a detailed recipe for lamb stew from a woman who had just found her husband in the bath with a kitchen knife. Nothing surprised him anymore, which was useful, because it meant nothing showed.

Tom heard him out without expression. Then he said no.

Francois asked why.

Tom didn't answer that. He looked at Francois the way a doctor looks at a symptom — clinically, carefully, without the patient knowing what's being measured.

What he didn't say — what he wouldn't say to a client, or a stranger, or frankly anyone — was that he'd been hearing things for the better part of a year. Whispers, mostly. The kind that travel through the back channels of the city, cop to cop, case to cold case, spoken quietly over bad coffee in rooms with the door closed.

Something was going around. Something that got into people. Not a sickness, not exactly — more like a *reorientation.* Men and women who by all accounts had been perfectly ordinary, perfectly decent, until one day they simply weren't anymore. The ones who could still talk afterwards struggled to describe the before and after. The ones who couldn't talk anymore were the ones who kept Tom up at night.

He glanced at Francois — the too-wide eyes, the hands clasped just a little too tight.

The city had been changing. Tom had felt it — a low hum beneath the surface of ordinary life, like a frequency just below hearing. Shops closing early. Neighbours who stopped making eye contact. The woman at the corner deli who had always known his order and then one Tuesday didn't recognise him at all, and smiled when she said so, and the smile was wrong.

He'd noticed more padlocks, more drawn curtains, more people walking fast after dark. And running through all of it, like a thread through a bad quilt, the whispers. Always the whispers. Something was moving through the city, and nobody could point at it directly, and everybody knew.

He'd steered clear of anything that smelled like it for months. And something about the shape of this case — a senseless death, a man clinging to furniture, eyes that were just slightly too wide — smelled exactly like it.

So he said no. He said it once, clearly, the way you close a door. He expected Francois to leave.

But Francois didn't leave. He stayed in the chair across from Tom's desk, and something in his posture shifted — not desperation, not anger, something more deliberate. Like a man changing gears.

He was persistent. He leaned in, voice low and urgent, chipping away — planting small seeds of doubt, little splinters of argument that lodged themselves just under the skin — "What if the police botch it? What if justice is never served? Where is the hope, and where is the faith? What if the truth just disappears, like she did?"[^angels]

He stopped. "Don't try too hard to think," he said. "Don't —"[^staring] He paused. Seemed to lose it briefly. "If there's an order in all of this disorder — I just need to know what it is."[^wake]

Then, just for a moment — half a second, maybe less — something crossed his face. Not grief, not urgency. Something that looked, just briefly, almost like terror. Then it was gone, and he was back, hands clasped, eyes searching Tom's face like nothing had happened.

Tom filed it away — the thing he couldn't name. He'd seen a lot of faces in twenty years. Grief had a look. Fear had a look. Guilt had a look. What had just crossed Francois's face wasn't any of them. It was the look of something trapped — something behind the face, trying to get out, failing, and being pulled back under.

He'd spent twenty years developing an instinct, and the instinct was making noise.

Francois said all the right things in all the right order, and his devastation seemed genuine, and none of that stopped the quiet alarm going off somewhere below Tom's conscious mind. A man in genuine shock didn't argue this well. A man in genuine shock didn't land his points this cleanly.

Tom had the distinct and unverifiable sense of being steered.

The other thing — smaller, harder to name — was the rhythm of some of it. The phrasing. Every so often Francois would say something and it would scan differently — like he was receiving the words rather than choosing them. Too even. A little too complete. Like a line from a song, almost — like it had a life that predated the conversation it arrived in.

He should have walked away. He knew he should have walked away. He'd walked away from easier cases for less reason. A missing dog in the suburbs. A cheating husband where the wife already had the photographs. Cases that had nothing wrong with them except the faint, sub-audible hum of something he didn't want to touch. And here was a case radiating that hum like a tuning fork, and he was still sitting in his chair.

"He just needs to know who did this," Francois said. "Francois deserves that much."

Tom was quiet for a while after Francois said that. The third person. *Francois deserves that much.* Not *I deserve that much.* As though the man sitting in the chair and the man he was talking about were two different people, and he'd forgotten, just for a moment, which one he was.

Tom sat with his fist pressed hard against his chin, eyes fixed on the middle distance. Two alarm systems going off at once. The instinct. The rumors. He turned the shape of the thing over, looking for the angle that held, looking for the solid ground of reason in a conversation that kept moving the floor on him.

![Tom fist to chin](../../images/600-ice-48004.jpg)

Finally, something gave.

"Fine," he said. "But I do this my way."

Francois nodded. He stood. He extended a hand. Tom looked at it. Shook it. The grip was dry and firm and lasted exactly as long as a handshake should — no longer, no shorter — and that, too, was filed away. A man who had just hired a detective to investigate the murder of someone he loved should not have had that much control over his motor functions. Grief makes you clumsy. Whatever Francois was running on, it wasn't grief.

The door closed behind him. The stairs creaked this time — both of them, in order, announcing the departure the way they'd failed to announce the arrival. Tom sat in the quiet for a while. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at the wall. He looked at the frosted glass with his name on it and thought about how a name on a door was supposed to mean something — that behind this glass was a man of a certain kind, reliable, dependable, the kind who followed the evidence wherever it went.

He was not at all sure he wanted to follow this evidence wherever it went.

He took the case anyway. He'd said he would, and he was the kind of man who did what he said he would, which is the most dangerous kind of man there is, because it means other people can predict you.

He locked the office. Checked the deadbolt — twice, this time, though he couldn't have said why. The stairs creaked under his feet as he went down, every step announcing itself the way it was supposed to. The street was dark. A few cars. A streetlight buzzing. The dry cleaner's was closed, its window showing a rack of plastic-wrapped shirts standing in a line like soldiers waiting for orders that would never come.

He walked home. It took twenty minutes. He didn't think about the case. He thought about the city — the way it looked at night, the way it sounded, the way the streetlights made everything the same shade of amber. He thought about the whispers he'd been hearing for a year and about how the city had looked exactly like this twelve months ago and yet felt completely different, in a way that defied measurement, in a way that was easy to dismiss if you wanted to dismiss it, and Tom had wanted to dismiss it, and he had, and now he couldn't.

## 3

Tom worked the case for three days straight. He followed every thread until the threads led somewhere ugly. And they always led somewhere ugly.

The first day was people. He started early — six thirty, the city still grey, the streets still wet from overnight rain that had finally made good on its threat. He drank his coffee standing up, put on his coat, and went to knock on doors.

Vivian's neighbour, Mrs Halford, answered the door with the chain still on and one eye visible through the gap. She said she hadn't heard anything the night Vivian died. She said she hadn't seen anyone come or go. She said this in a flat, careful voice, the chain still taut, and when Tom asked if Francois had seemed different lately she looked at him for a very long time before she said, "Different how?"

Tom said he didn't know exactly. Something in the manner, maybe. The way a person talks.

The chain rattled. "I don't listen to how people talk," Mrs Halford said. "Not anymore." And she closed the door.

Tom stood in the hallway for a moment after it shut. The building smelled like old carpet and cooked onions and the particular variety of loneliness that collects in places where people live close together and keep to themselves. He could hear a television through one wall, a radio through another. Behind Mrs Halford's door, nothing. Just the silence of a woman who had decided, at some point in the recent past, that not listening was the safest available option.

He went downstairs. The morning was brighter now, the grey lifting slightly, and the street was beginning its slow, reluctant transition into day. A delivery truck double-parked outside a grocer's. Two kids on bicycles. A man in a brown coat walking a dog that didn't want to be walked. The city, doing its impression of a city where nothing was wrong.

He talked to Barker, who ran the newsstand on the corner of Francois's block.

Barker was the kind of man who noticed everything and admitted to nothing. He had a face like a crumpled receipt and hands that were always busy — folding, stacking, making change, straightening the rows of papers that nobody bought anymore. Tom had bought papers from him for twelve years, and that counted for something. Not much. But something.

Barker said Francois had been fine — normal, friendly, regular hours — and then about two months ago he'd stopped coming in. Not gradually. Just stopped. One day he was there, the next day he wasn't, and the day after that wasn't either. Barker had assumed he'd moved, or taken ill, or found a different newsstand, and any of those possibilities was equally acceptable to a man who sold papers for a living.

"Then about three weeks back he came in again," Barker said. "Bought the usual. Stood right there." He pointed at the spot. "And he said something I've been thinking about since."

"What was that?"

Barker hesitated. He folded a newspaper in half, then in half again, working the crease with his thumbnail. "He said, 'You never knew love until you crossed the line of grace.'"[^please] He looked up. "Just like that. Like he was reading it off a card. I said, 'Come again?' and he smiled — and I've known this man for years, Tom, and I'm telling you, the smile was new — and he said never mind, and he left."

Tom wrote it down. He stood at the newsstand and looked at the spot where Francois had stood, and tried to see what Barker had seen — a man he'd known for years, saying words that belonged to someone else, with a smile that didn't fit his face. The spot was just a spot. Pavement. Gum stains. A crack running diagonally toward the kerb. Whatever had stood there three weeks ago had left nothing visible behind.

But it had left Barker folding newspapers and thinking about it since. That counted for something. The corruption, whatever it was, left a wake. People noticed. They just didn't know what they were noticing.

The bartender at The Anchor, where Vivian had gone on Fridays, said she'd come in alone the last three weeks. She'd always come with Francois before. She'd sat at the bar and ordered her usual and not spoken to anyone. The bartender said she'd seemed — and here he paused, cleaning a glass, looking for the word — *careful.* Like someone walking on ice.

"She ever say anything about Francois?" Tom asked.

The bartender thought about it. He was a large man with a small moustache and the measured patience of someone who spends eight hours a night listening to other people's problems. He polished the glass in slow circles, thinking.

"Once," he said. "Last Friday. She was on her second drink, which was unusual — she was usually a one-and-done. She sat there for a while, turning the glass, not drinking, just — holding it. Then she said, 'Do you ever hear something you can't unhear?'"

He set the glass down.

"I thought she was talking about the jukebox. I said, 'Every night of my life, love.' She didn't laugh. She looked at me like I'd said something sad, which I hadn't, or at least I hadn't meant to." He picked up another glass. "She left her drink half-finished. That's the thing I keep coming back to. She was not the type to leave a drink half-finished."

Tom talked to a few others. The woman who ran the laundromat two doors down said she'd seen nothing and heard nothing and had a manner that suggested she intended to continue seeing and hearing nothing for the foreseeable future. A man in the flat below Francois's said the pipes had been making a sound, a sort of low humming, and was that relevant? Tom said he didn't think so but wrote it down anyway, because you never knew, and because the man looked like he needed someone to write something down.

A retired postman named Lyle had known Vivian for years and Francois for almost as long. Lyle was the most forthcoming. He sat on his front steps with a dog at his feet and told Tom that Vivian had come to see him about ten days before she died. She'd stood right where Tom was standing. She'd asked Lyle if he thought people could change — really change, not just grow or age but become someone fundamentally different. Lyle had said he supposed they could. Vivian had nodded, as though that confirmed something she'd been afraid of, and thanked him, and left.

"She didn't say who she was talking about," Lyle said. "She didn't have to." He looked down at the dog, which had fallen asleep. "I should've done something. I don't know what. Called someone. Asked more questions. Something." He paused. "But you don't, do you? You think about it, and you tell yourself it's not your business, and you wait, and then it's too late, and you spend the rest of your life thinking about a woman standing on your steps asking whether people can change."

Everyone he talked to said Vivian had been worried about Francois for weeks. Nobody had done anything about it, including her. The city had a talent for that — for knowing something was wrong and waiting for someone else to fix it. Tom was beginning to suspect that talent was not entirely accidental.

He went back to his office that night and spread his notes across the desk. Four witnesses. Four variations on the same story: something had changed, nobody had intervened, and now Vivian Plume was dead. He stared at the notes until the words stopped being words and became shapes on paper — just ink, just lines, no meaning — and then he blinked and they reassembled and the meaning was worse than before.

He poured himself a drink. He didn't drink it. He sat with it in his hand and looked at the wall and thought about a city full of people who had noticed something was wrong and done nothing, and about the fact that he was one of them, and about the fact that he was doing something now, and about whether *now* was the right word or whether *too late* was closer to the truth.

The second day, Tom went through the rooms.

He let himself in with the key Francois had given him — a small brass thing on a plain ring, handed over without ceremony. The building was quiet at that hour. Mid-morning on a Tuesday. The other tenants at work, or out, or behind their own doors doing whatever people do on Tuesdays when the world is ending slowly and they haven't been told. The stairwell smelled like floor polish. The numbers on the doors were brass, slightly tarnished, the kind of detail that tells you a building was once cared for and now merely maintained.

The lock turned smoothly. The door opened onto a hallway that still held the faint chemical smell of the forensic team's visit, already fading under the older, more permanent smell of the place itself — fabric softener, dust, the ghost of a hundred cooked meals.

The bed with its missing occupant. A boombox. Scissors. A coffee cup. Three days of unopened mail. The apartment had the airless quality of a place where something had stopped — not just a life, but the mechanism of ordinary living. Dishes in the sink, half washed. A book open face-down on the arm of a chair, the spine cracked at page forty-two, as though someone had set it down meaning to come back. A window open an inch, letting in a thin line of city noise that made the silence louder.

Tom moved through the rooms slowly, touching nothing at first, just looking. He'd learned this from an older detective, years ago — a man named Purcell who'd retired before the city started changing and who would not have recognised the place it had become. Purcell had said: the first pass is eyes only. You walk through and you let the room tell you what it wants you to see. Then you walk through again and you look at what it doesn't.

On the second pass, he touched things. Opened drawers. Checked behind cushions. Looked under the bed, where there was nothing but dust and a single hair clip, turquoise, that must have been Vivian's. He held it in his palm for a moment, then set it back where he'd found it.

He went through it all. Expecting nothing. What he found was a folded piece of paper in her desk drawer — not a diary, not a letter. A list. Eight or nine lines in her handwriting, each one something Francois had said. No dates. No context. Just the words, because the words had been strange enough to write down. Most of them Tom couldn't place. But near the bottom, underlined twice, with a question mark beside it: *You know you're chewing bubble gum.*[^discotheque]

He looked at it for a long time. He thought about a woman writing down the things her partner said because they had started to frighten her — not knowing why, not knowing what to do with the list once she had it. He thought about the better part of a year he'd spent listening to the city whisper about something that rewrote people quietly, patiently, until one day the words coming out of their mouths weren't theirs anymore.

He sat at her desk and read the list again, line by line. Some of it was fragments — half sentences, words trailing off into nothing. One line read only: *Looking for the face I had before the world was made.*[^mofo] Vivian had drawn a small line under it and nothing else. No question mark this time. As though she'd understood that one perfectly and wished she hadn't.

He folded the paper and put it in his pocket.

He turned to the boombox.

It sat on the bed like an altar. A U2 CD loaded in the tray — the disc itself half-ejected, as though someone had tried to remove it and then thought better of it, or been unable to complete the action. The label faced up. *Pop.* U2. 1997. Tom stood over it, not moving. The room was very quiet. Through the open inch of window he could hear the city — traffic, a distant siren, the low hum of ten thousand lives going on as normal — and in here there was nothing but the boombox and the silence it was holding.

He knew this smell. Not literally — there was no smell. But the instinct he'd spent twenty years building knew what it was standing next to. He knew what the boombox was. He knew what the boombox had done. He knew what pressing play would mean.

His hand moved before he'd decided.

The sound came through small and tinny — the boombox speakers were cheap, blown at the edges — and for the first few seconds it was just music. Just noise. And then it wasn't. Something in it shifted, or something in him shifted to meet it, and he felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. Not in his ears. Behind them. A presence, mapping him, taking his measure. Quiet and patient and very, very sure of itself.

He listened for forty seconds. Then he pressed stop and stood very still for a long moment.

*So it was true.*

His hand was shaking. He noticed this with the clinical detachment of a man observing his own symptoms. He set it flat on the edge of the bed and waited until it stopped. The room was very quiet. The city noise through the open window sounded different now — further away, thinner, as though a pane of something had been placed between him and the ordinary world. He knew that feeling was not real. He knew it was the afterimage of what he'd just heard, the way a bright light leaves a shape on your retina that isn't there. He knew this the way he knew his own name.

He also knew, with equal certainty, that knowing it didn't make it go away. That the thing he'd felt in those forty seconds — the presence, the mapping, the quiet intelligence taking his measure — was not something that stopped when you pressed the button. It stopped playing. That didn't mean it stopped working.

He didn't press play again.

![Stripes on the boombox](../../images/600-ice-48002.jpg)

The third day was the hardest, because the third day was thinking.

Tom sat in his office with the door locked and the blinds drawn and the list from Vivian's desk spread flat on the blotter. Outside, the city went about its business. He could hear the dry cleaner's below — the hiss of the press, the clink of hangers on the rail, the muffled voices of people dropping off shirts and picking them up and never once looking at the staircase that led to the second floor. He'd always liked that about the location. Nobody looked up. Nobody wondered what was above the dry cleaner's. It was the kind of anonymity a detective needed.

Today it felt different. Today it felt less like anonymity and more like being forgotten.

He lined up everything he knew and made himself look at it.

The picture became clear. Francois had found the album. Had listened — once, probably, just to see what all the fuss was about. That's how it always started. Just once. And then the music had gotten into him, into the soft tissue behind his eyes, and it had begun its quiet, patient work. Rewriting things. Darkening corners. Turning a reasonable man into something that could look at Vivian — innocent Vivian — and decide she knew too much.

Because she did know. She'd been there when he first listened. She'd seen what it was doing to him. She'd watched the man she knew begin to say things the man she knew would never say, and she'd written them down, and she'd gone to The Anchor alone on Fridays, and she'd asked Lyle whether people could really change. And at some point the thing that used to be Francois had looked at her — at the woman who had sat beside him and listened and written things down in blue ink — and decided she knew too much. And a corrupted mind cannot abide a witness.

The words on her list — Tom could see them now for what they were. Not Francois's words. The album's words. Fragments of lyrics bleeding through a man's speech like water through a cracked wall. She'd heard them and known something was wrong. She'd written them down because writing them down was the only thing she could do. And it hadn't saved her.

He thought about the bartender saying *she was not the type to leave a drink half-finished.* He thought about Lyle on his front steps, Vivian asking him whether people could truly change. He thought about Mrs Halford's chain on the door, and how a whole neighbourhood had watched something happen to Francois and done nothing except, in their own small ways, pull back. Close up. Lock the door. Stop listening to how people talk.

Vivian hadn't pulled back. Vivian had kept a list. Vivian had kept coming home to a man whose words weren't his own anymore, and she'd stayed, and she'd watched, and she'd written things down in her careful handwriting, and she'd underlined the ones that frightened her most, and she'd died.

Tom sat with that for a while. He owed her the time. Outside, a siren passed — distant, fading, heading somewhere else. The dry cleaner's press hissed. The city went on. Vivian Plume did not go on. That was the central, irreducible fact of the case, and every thread Tom had followed in three days led back to it.

The conclusion was impossible. Tom ran the logic again. And again. Same answer, every time. He'd been doing this long enough to know the difference between a conclusion you don't want and a conclusion that's wrong. This one he didn't want. It wasn't wrong.

He sat with it for another hour, turning it, testing the joints, looking for the weak point that would let it fall apart. There wasn't one. The evidence interlocked with the precision of something engineered — each piece supporting the next, each witness corroborating the last, the boombox at the centre of it like a keystone in an arch. Remove any one element and the rest held. That was either the hallmark of truth or the hallmark of something designed to look like truth, and in twenty years of detective work Tom had never found a reliable way to tell the two apart.

He stood up. Folded the list. Put on his coat.

He went back to Francois.

---

They sat across from each other at a small table in a room with no decoration and one window. The light was the thin, grey kind that flatters nothing. Francois looked smaller than he had in Tom's office — diminished somehow, as though the days since the hiring had cost him something. His hands were on the table, palms down, very still. He watched Tom the way an animal watches a sound it can't identify.

Tom didn't rush it. He'd learned, over the years, that the beginning of an interrogation was less about what you said than about the quality of the silence you established first. He let it build. Let Francois sit in it. Let the room fill with the weight of everything that was about to be said.

Then he laid out what he knew, piece by piece, watching Francois's face for the cracks.

He started with the neighbours. The two months of absence. The changed smile. Barker's account — the words spoken like lines read off a card. Mrs Halford's chain and her careful, flat refusal to engage. Lyle's story of Vivian standing on his steps, asking whether people could truly change, and the look on her face when she got her answer.

He laid down Vivian's list — unfolded it carefully on the table between them, the handwriting facing Francois. He watched Francois look at it. Watched something tighten behind his eyes — a micro-movement, a flinch at the cellular level. Watched his hands, still flat on the table, press down slightly harder, as though the table might move. He watched for guilt and didn't find it. He watched for shock and didn't find that either. What he found was recognition — the look of a man seeing something he already knew, presented in a form he hadn't expected. The list was Vivian's last act of agency, and Francois recognised it for what it was, and the recognition told Tom everything the confession hadn't.

He talked about the boombox. The U2 CD in the tray. What forty seconds had felt like — the way it moved, the patient intelligence of it, the sense of something mapping you from the inside. He talked about the city, the rumours, the year of whispers that had led him in a slow, tightening circle to this room, this table, this conversation.

Francois listened to all of it without interrupting. His face gave nothing away, which in itself gave something away — because three days ago that face had been a theatre of grief and urgency, and now it was a wall, and walls go up for a reason.

Tom paused. He let the silence do its work. He'd been in enough interrogation rooms to know that silence was a better tool than any question — that most people, left alone with the shape of what they'd done, would eventually start talking just to fill the space. The guilty ones especially. The guilty ones couldn't stand the quiet, because the quiet was where the truth lived, and they'd spent so long building walls around it that hearing it breathe was unbearable.

Francois didn't fidget. Didn't look away. Didn't fill the silence. He sat perfectly still, hands flat on the table, and waited, and the waiting had the quality of something mechanical — not patience but the absence of impatience, which was worse.

The cracks came slowly, then all at once. Francois looked out the window at the dark nothing beyond the glass.

Then his shoulders dropped.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I did it."

The room changed. Not physically — the walls were the same, the light was the same, the thin grey window was the same. But something in the air shifted, the way it shifts when a door opens onto a room you hadn't known was there. Tom had heard confessions before. Dozens of them. They all felt like this — like the moment a lock turns and you realise the door was never as solid as it looked.

He turned to face Tom.

"I strangled her. No emotional good night."[^gone]

![Stripes strangling Vivian Plume](../../images/600-ice-48007.jpg)

Tom said nothing.

"Why?"

Francois looked at the window. "She felt the ground was giving way," he said simply. "Better off that way."[^lastnight]

Tom looked at him for a moment. The answer had the shape of something rehearsed — too finished, arrived too easily. Like the arguments in his office. Like the handshake at the door. Everything Francois said had that quality — of having been prepared in advance, as though the words had been waiting in a drawer and Francois had simply opened it at the right moment and taken out the correct one. It was the fluency of a man who had stopped having to think about what he was saying, because something else was doing the thinking for him.

He filed it next to the other things he couldn't name. The file was getting crowded.

He already knew, of course. He'd pressed play. He'd stood in that apartment with the half-washed dishes and the open book and the turquoise hair clip under the bed, and he'd felt the corruption move through him like cold water — forty seconds of it, quiet and patient and rewriting things. He understood exactly what had entered Francois's mind. He understood it the way you understand a burn after you've touched the stove — not from theory, not from description, but from the brief, searing fact of contact.

And then, because he was the kind of detective who couldn't leave a loose thread, he asked the question that had been sitting at the back of his mind since the very first day. The question he'd carried through three days of interviews and evidence and a list in a dead woman's handwriting. The question that had been there since the moment Francois walked into his office without tripping the deadbolt or creaking the stairs.

He let the silence hold for a moment. Then:

"Then why," he said slowly, "did you hire me?"

The question sat in the room between them like a third person. Tom watched it land. Watched Francois receive it.

Francois said nothing. He turned to the window and stared through it at nothing in particular.

Tom watched him. He thought about the office — the urgency, the arguments, the pressure applied in exactly the right places. He'd thought it then. He thought it again now: a man in genuine shock didn't argue that well. A man in genuine shock didn't land his points that cleanly.

He thought about the flash of terror that had crossed Francois's face and vanished before it could be named — not grief, not guilt, something more like a man catching a glimpse of something inside himself that frightened him.

He thought about a man who had referred to himself in the third person and hadn't seemed to notice, as though one part of him didn't fully recognise the other as itself.

And then he thought about forty seconds of music moving through him. He thought about what that same thing, given more time, given *much* more time — given enough time to finish what it started — might do to the architecture of a mind.

Whether it would hollow a man out all at once, or whether it would take him room by room, leaving some parts intact while it worked on the others.

And if some part was left intact — smaller, dimmer, running out of time — it would know what had happened. It would know what the rest of the mind had done.

It would carry that knowledge the way a man carries a wound — silently, with the walls closing in and the lights going out one by one and the sound of the music getting louder in the rooms it had already taken.

It would need someone to find out. But it couldn't just walk into a police station. The rest of the mind wouldn't allow it. It couldn't write a letter, couldn't make a call, couldn't do anything the corrupted majority would notice and prevent.

So it would have to find another way in.

It would have to convince the corrupted mind that hiring a detective was a good idea. Frame it as strategy, not confession. *Let him come. Let him find the boombox. Let him press play. Then he'll be one of us.*

The corrupted mind would like that logic. It would agree. And by the time it realised the trap — that the detective was good enough to find the truth, that the fragment had been counting on exactly that — it would be too late.

Unless it wasn't a trap at all. Unless the fragment had simply lost its grip and stumbled into the only door it could find, not knowing if it led to rescue or to ruin. Whether Francois had been calculating or merely desperate was the kind of question that answered itself differently depending on the hour.

Tom looked at Francois. Francois looked at the window. Between them, the table with the list, the evidence of a woman's fear laid out in blue ink and careful handwriting, and the entire weight of a case that had begun with a man clinging to a bedpost and ended here, in this room, with this silence. Three days of legwork. Four witnesses. A boombox. Forty seconds. And a logic that was elegant and terrible and explained everything including the thing Tom most wished it didn't — which was why he'd taken the case in the first place.

Tom opened his mouth.

Francois turned from the window. He looked at Tom's face. Whatever he saw there — the completed logic, the final piece dropping into place, the sentence forming behind Tom's eyes — he didn't wait for it to arrive.

He reached for the nearest weapon.

The scissors.

*Of course it was the scissors.*

![Stripes wielding scissors](../../images/600-ice-48003.jpg)

## 4

Tom moved fast. Faster than a man with a zebra on his head had any right to. They collided — Francois lunging, Tom blocking, the scissors clattering sideways as they grappled across the room in a tangle of flannel and stripes and raw desperation. The table went over. The chair followed. Something glass hit the floor and didn't survive the experience.

Francois was stronger than he looked — or the thing driving him was stronger than Francois, which amounted to the same result. He fought like a man who had nothing left to protect and nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous kind of man there is. There was no technique in it, no calculation — just force and fury, a body throwing itself at the only available obstacle. Tom had size on him and twenty years of knowing when to duck, and even so it was closer than he'd ever admit to anyone afterwards. He caught an elbow to the jaw that he felt in his molars. He took a knee to the ribs that would leave a bruise shaped like a small country.

Francois screamed — something torn and broken, something between a cry and an answer. "I'm not coming down, I'm not coming down!"[^gone] Tom held on. They spun. They crashed. The zebra, impossibly, remained on Tom's head throughout.

![Tom screaming](../../images/600-ice-48001.jpg)

---

Francois's legs went out from under him — the second time that week, and this time there was nothing to hold onto. He went down hard. Tom caught him before he hit the floor, or most of him, and eased him the rest of the way down. He crouched beside him. Both of them breathing hard. The room was wrecked — table on its side, chair in the corner, broken glass catching the overhead light in small, sharp pieces. Neither of them looked at it.

Tom checked the scissors. They were under the overturned table, half-hidden by a sheaf of scattered papers. He left them there. He checked Francois — conscious, breathing, not reaching for anything. The fight had gone out of him with the same sudden completeness as the confession, as though whatever had been driving the attack had simply switched off.

Tom waited until his own breathing steadied. Then he said what he'd been about to say before the scissors interrupted.

"You couldn't confess," he said. "But part of you needed someone to find out."

Francois said nothing for a moment. He lay on his back on the floor, looking at the ceiling, his chest rising and falling. A bruise was forming on his left arm where Tom had pinned him. He didn't seem to notice.

Then something shifted in him — not grief, not defiance. He almost laughed.

"I'm already gone," he said. "Felt that way all along."[^gone]

Tom looked at him for a long time. It wasn't a denial. It wasn't a confirmation. It was the only answer the situation had left room for.

Something moved behind Francois's eyes — like a light seen from a very long way away. A signal from the last room. A window in a house that was otherwise dark.

Then it was gone.

## 5

Some time later, Francois lay on a table, strapped at the wrists and ankles.

Tom didn't know how much time had passed. Enough for the paperwork. Enough for Francois to be moved from the room with the overturned table and the broken glass to this room, which had no table, no glass, no sharp objects of any kind. Enough for Tom to stand in a corridor and drink a cup of bad coffee and stare at a wall and not think, deliberately and with effort, about any of the things he would eventually have to think about.

The room beyond the observation window was small and clinical. A metal stand beside the table. A single overhead light that turned everything the colour of old paper. The walls were painted a green that might once have been intended as calming and had instead achieved a kind of institutional despair. Tom stood on the other side of the glass, hands in his pockets, watching. He thought about a cornered thing finding a crack. A last lit room at the end of a long dark corridor, doing the only thing it could with the time it had left.

Vivian was dead. That was real, and it was terrible, and nothing that came after would change it. A woman with a teal collar and a list in her desk drawer and the habit of not leaving drinks unfinished. A woman who had noticed the words changing and stayed anyway. Tom thought about the turquoise hair clip under the bed. He thought about the book on the chair arm, open to page forty-two, waiting for a reader who would never come back. Small details. The kind that don't make it into the case file. The kind that stay with you.

But the boombox was in an evidence locker, sealed, tagged, going nowhere. The album had been censored. Nobody else would find it on a shelf somewhere and think *just once, just to see.* The corruption ended here, in this room, with this man on this table.

Someone had made sure of that. The question of who — the man, or the thing the music had made of him — was one Tom suspected he'd be turning over for a long time. He'd solved the case. He'd found the killer. He'd found the weapon — not the scissors, not the hands, but the thing that had put the intention behind them. By any measure, the job was done. And yet standing here, watching Francois through the glass, he could not shake the feeling that the case had solved him just as much as he'd solved it. That he'd been a tool. That the entire investigation, from the moment Francois walked into his office to the moment the scissors hit the floor, had been a mechanism designed to produce exactly this outcome — and whether the designer was the corruption or its last remaining enemy was the one question the mechanism had been built not to answer.

Francois turned his head. Found Tom's eyes through the glass.

And smiled.

He lifted one small striped arm and waved, slow and cheerful, with the complete serenity of a man at peace with every single choice he had ever made. The strap caught him short; the thin tube taped to the crook of his elbow shifted slightly with the motion. When he spoke, his voice came through the speaker above the glass, flat and close, like a voice in a small room.

"I don't regret a thing. It was all worth it. I'd do anything — anything — to hear that sound again."

He said it the way a man talks about the love of his life. Not with passion — passion burns and dies. With certainty. The deep, geological certainty of a man who has found the only thing that matters and cannot imagine a world without it. Or the certainty of a man performing certainty, because the performance was the point, because the last fragment of the real Francois needed Tom to walk away believing the corruption had won completely, so that nobody would ever think to press play and check.

There was no way to tell. That was the architecture of the thing.

His eyes found Tom's through the glass and stayed there. "You pressed play," he said. "Forty seconds." A pause. The smile didn't change. "There's an insect in your ear," he said. "If you scratch it, it won't disappear."[^staring]

Tom looked at him until he was done looking. It took a while. You don't look away from something like that quickly. You look at it the way you look at a fire or a wreck — because it demands to be seen, because turning away feels like the wrong kind of mercy.

The corruption, revelling to the end. Or a man who had found the only door left open to him, walked through it, and needed Tom to believe the corruption had been in charge the whole time.

Impossible to know. That was the point. The ambiguity wasn't a flaw in the case — it was the case's final, load-bearing structure. Remove it and the whole thing collapses into something simple, and it was not simple, and it was never going to be simple, and Tom was going to carry it with him for the rest of his life the way you carry a stone in your shoe that you can't quite find and can't quite forget.

Tom turned and walked away down the grey corridor. His footsteps echoed on the linoleum — flat, regular, the sound of a man leaving. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, one of them flickering in a slow, irregular rhythm that he couldn't quite stop noticing. The corridor was long. Longer than he remembered. Doors on both sides, all of them closed, each one with a small wire-reinforced window showing empty rooms. The building was quiet in the way that institutional buildings are quiet — not the absence of sound but the suppression of it, as though the walls had been designed to keep things in.

He passed a window at the end of the hall. Outside, the city was doing what the city always did — moving, humming, carrying on. Streetlights coming on in the early dusk. Traffic below, headlights in both directions, a slow arterial pulse. From up here it looked ordinary. It looked fine. It looked like a city that had not spent the better part of a year having something move through it that turned people into strangers. He thought, for no reason he could name, that the whole world could just dissolve into a glass of water[^velvet] — and then he thought, *where did that come from,* and kept walking.

And as he walked, another thought settled over him — quiet, patient, the way the worst thoughts always are. The rumors he'd been hearing for the better part of a year. The reorientation. The ordinary people who became something else. He had always imagined the corrupting force as something out there — something he'd managed to avoid, something he'd been careful around.

But he'd pressed play. Forty seconds. And he'd told himself that was nothing, that he'd stopped in time, that he'd felt it move through him and come out the other side clean. He'd told himself this with the absolute conviction of a man who needed it to be true, and he'd moved on to the next step of the investigation, and he hadn't thought about it again until Francois had said *You pressed play* through the speaker above the glass, and the world had shifted an inch to the left and stayed there.

Forty seconds. That was all. Forty seconds and a hand that moved before he'd decided, and a body that had walked toward the boombox while his mind was still calculating the risk, and the small matter of a detective who had spent twenty years trusting his instincts and then, on the one occasion that mattered most, ignored both of them at once.

He thought about the instinct that had told him to walk away — twenty years of detective work making noise he hadn't listened to. The rumors. Two alarm systems, both going off at once.

He thought *you're not the only one staring at the sun — afraid of what you'd find if you stepped back inside.*[^staring]

He thought: he had not walked away.

He thought about Barker's face at the newsstand, the way the man had folded and refolded the newspaper while he talked, the way he'd said *the smile was new.* He thought about Mrs Halford's eye through the chain-gap, the flatness of her voice, the way she'd said *I don't listen to how people talk. Not anymore.* The city had been learning, person by person, to stop asking questions. To look away. To close the door and fasten the chain and wait for whatever was happening to happen to someone else.

He'd told himself he was different. Twenty years of instinct. A darker mind. A man who stared into the abyss. He'd told himself this with the easy confidence of a man who had never been tested, and now the test had come, and the results were in, and he was walking down a grey corridor with someone else's lyrics drifting through his thoughts like smoke through an open window.

He thought about Purcell — the old detective, the one who'd taught him to let a room speak before he searched it. Purcell had retired years ago. Gone somewhere warm. Sent a postcard once, then nothing. Tom wondered now whether Purcell had retired or whether Purcell had seen this coming — the thing that was moving through the city — and had simply gotten out while getting out was still an option.

But Francois had walked through his door without tripping a single alarm, and Tom had let him in, and he'd taken the case, and he'd pressed play. Every step logical. Every step chosen freely. And every step leading exactly where the corrupted mind — or the uncorrupted fragment — had needed him to go.

The question was which one had been driving.

The other question — the one he was only now arriving at, the one that had been waiting for him at the end of this corridor the way a cold front waits at the edge of a warm day — was whether it mattered. Whether there was a meaningful difference between a man who chose freely and a man who had been reoriented so gently that the choosing still felt free. Whether *I pressed stop after forty seconds* and *something let me press stop after forty seconds* were the same sentence or two different ones. Whether the instinct he'd trusted for twenty years was his instinct, or whether it had been quietly, patiently replaced by something that felt identical and wanted different things.

He kept walking. His footsteps echoed. The fluorescent light flickered. At the far end of the corridor, an exit sign glowed red, the only colour in a building that seemed to have been designed in shades of grey.

He was almost at the exit when he stopped. Middle of the corridor. Hands in his pockets. Staring at the grey nothing ahead of him.

He didn't move. The exit sign hummed. The fluorescent light flickered. Somewhere deeper in the building, a door closed — distant, soft, final.

Those weren't his words. He knew it the same way you know a foreign coin in your pocket before you look at it — by its weight, by its edge, by the slight wrongness of it against your fingers. He tried to trace the thought back to where it had come from.

Nothing. A gap where the source should be.

He thought about Francois saying *You know you're chewing bubble gum* and not knowing where the sentence came from. He thought about Vivian underlining it and adding a question mark. He thought about a woman who had noticed the words changing and written them down and it hadn't been enough.

He thought about his own hand reaching for the play button before his mind had given the order. About how easy it had been. About how natural it had felt. About the small, unexamined decision to stand over the boombox in the first place, and the smaller, more unexamined decision to put his hand near the controls, and the smallest decision of all — the one that wasn't a decision — to press play.

He thought about Vivian's list. About a woman writing down the things that frightened her. About whether, somewhere in his own mind, there were things he should be writing down and wasn't.

He thought: *I told myself forty seconds was nothing.*

He thought about the word *reorientation* and how he'd always applied it to other people. To cases on his desk. To the city's problem. To the whispers over bad coffee in rooms with the door closed. Never to the man in the mirror. Never to the man walking down this corridor with his hands in his pockets and somebody else's words in his head. Never to the detective who took the case he should have walked away from, who pressed play on the boombox he knew not to touch, who sat in his office above the dry cleaner's and told himself he was different because his instincts were good, and whose instincts had led him here — to a grey corridor, to a question without an answer, to the sound of his own footsteps echoing in a building designed to keep things in.

The question arrived slowly, the way a cold front arrives. Not a dramatic thing. Just a change in the air.

*What if forty seconds wasn't where it started?*

Behind him, the observation window went dark.

![Stripes under the blanket](../../images/600-ice-48000.jpg)

## 6

*Vivian Plume was buried on a Wednesday. It rained. The kind of rain that doesn't stop and doesn't try to. A small crowd, smaller than she deserved. Lyle was there. The bartender from The Anchor was there. Mrs Halford was not.*

*The boombox stays locked up. Evidence locker 14B, third shelf, sealed in a bag with a tag that says BIOHAZARD in letters large enough to discourage curiosity. Nobody has opened it. Nobody will.*

*Tom went back to his office above the dry cleaner's. He sat behind his desk. He looked at the frosted glass with his name on it and the deadbolt that hadn't caught and the stairs that hadn't creaked, and he thought about all the things he'd filed away and not thought about, and he wondered how many of them he'd filed away because he chose to, and how many because something had chosen for him.*

*Let this story serve as a warning.*
